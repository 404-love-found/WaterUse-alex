**I.  Action‑situations (decentralised – DV only)**  

Below are all *distinct* strategic tensions that arise when the nine farmers act **independently** (no national authority).  
Each situation is expressed in the Institutional Analysis & Development (IAD) terminology as a “action situation” and is illustrated with a **2‑player normal‑form payoff matrix**.  
The matrices are schematic – the numbers are illustrative only and are meant to capture the relative ranking of outcomes (higher = more desirable).  

---

### 1.  Water‑competition between an upstream (U) and a downstream (D) farmer  

|                     | **Downstream – Low (L)**<br>≤ 5 fields | **Downstream – High (H)**<br>≥ 6 fields |
|---------------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Upstream – Low (L)** | (3 , 3)  <br>*Both obtain enough water → high yields* | (3 , 2)  <br>*U gets enough (first‑come), D suffers a moderate shortage* |
| **Upstream – High (H)**| (4 , 1)  <br>*U extracts a lot, D receives very little* | (4 , 0)  <br>*U still gets plenty (first‑come), D gets none* |

**Why this is a distinct tension**  
*Strategic conflict over a **common‑pool water resource** where the order of extraction (spatial asymmetry) matters.*  
- The upstream farmer’s decision does **not** depend on the downstream farmer’s choice (he is “first‑in‑line”), but the downstream farmer’s payoff is highly sensitive to the upstream farmer’s irrigation level.  
- Both players face a trade‑off between **expanding fields** (higher immediate revenue) and **preserving water** for the downstream neighbour.  

---

### 2.  Fishing‑priority conflict (downstream priority)  

|                     | **Upstream – No‑fish (N)** | **Upstream – Fish (F)** |
|---------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Downstream – No‑fish (N)** | (0 , 0)  <br>*No one fishes → no catch* | (0 , 2)  <br>*U alone catches a modest amount* |
| **Downstream – Fish (F)**    | (2 , 3)  <br>*D fishes first, takes most of the target catch* | (1 , 3)  <br>*Both fish – D gets the bulk, U gets a small residual* |

**Why this is a distinct tension**  
*Strategic interaction over a **subsistence‑fish common‑pool** where **spatial priority** (downstream first) creates an asymmetric payoff.*  
- The downstream farmer can secure the bulk of the target catch simply by deciding to fish; the upstream farmer’s payoff depends on whether the downstream farmer fishes.  
- No monetary cost is attached to fishing, so the tension is purely about **access and timing**.  

---

### 3.  Upstream irrigation vs ecological threshold (water‑flow → fish recruitment)  

Here the second “player” is the **environmental state** (E) that can be either **Good (G)** – water flow above the larval‑survival threshold – or **Bad (B)** – flow below the threshold.  

|                     | **Environment – Good (G)** | **Environment – Bad (B)** |
|---------------------|----------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Upstream – Low irrigation (L)** | (3 , 3)  <br>*U gets decent yields, fish recruitment succeeds* | (1 , 0)  <br>*U suffers low yields, fish fail to recruit* |
| **Upstream – High irrigation (H)**| (4 , 1)  <br>*U maximises current yield, but reduces flow → poor recruitment* | (2 , 0)  <br>*Even with high extraction, the natural inflow is insufficient → low yield, no recruitment* |

**Why this is a distinct tension**  
*Strategic dilemma between **short‑term agricultural profit** and **long‑term ecological viability** of the fish population.*  
- The upstream farmer’s irrigation level directly influences whether the river flow stays above the **ecological threshold** needed for larval survival in May.  
- If the threshold is crossed, the fish stock collapses, reducing future fishing returns for **all** farmers (captured by the environment’s payoff).  

---

### 4.  Joint field‑expansion race (two neighbouring farmers, no hierarchy)  

Although water extraction is sequential, when **both farmers are located close together** (e.g., farms 3 and 4) they may anticipate that the upstream neighbour will also expand. The tension is a classic **“prisoner’s dilemma”** over field expansion.  

|                     | **Neighbour – Low (L)** | **Neighbour – High (H)** |
|---------------------|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Self – Low (L)**  | (3 , 3)  <br>*Both keep moderate fields → stable yields* | (2 , 4)  <br>*Self stays low, neighbour expands and captures extra water → self loses some yield* |
| **Self – High (H)** | (4 , 2)  <br>*Self expands, neighbour stays low → self gains* | (1 , 1)  <br>*Both expand → severe water stress for both* |

**Why this is a distinct tension**  
*Even without a formal hierarchy, the **mutual expectation** about field numbers creates a strategic trade‑off.*  
- Each farmer must decide whether to **pre‑emptively expand** to secure water before the neighbour does.  
- The payoff structure is symmetric, but the **spatial proximity** makes the decision interdependent.
