### 1️⃣ Action Situation A – *Up‑stream vs. Down‑stream Water Extraction (Crop‑Yield Conflict)*  

| **Players** | **Up‑stream farmer (U)** | **Down‑stream farmer (D)** |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Strategies** | **I** – *Increase* number of irrigated fields by +1 (risk‑seeking)  <br> **S** – *Stay* with current number of fields (conservative) | **I** – *Increase* number of irrigated fields by +1  <br> **S** – *Stay* with current number of fields |

#### Payoff Matrix  

|                | D‑I (increase) | D‑S (stay) |
|----------------|----------------|------------|
| **U‑I** (increase) | (H , L)  <br>U gets a high crop yield because water is still sufficient upstream; D suffers low yield – downstream water is depleted. | (M , M)  <br>Both receive moderate yields; enough water reaches downstream because upstream does not over‑extract. |
| **U‑S** (stay)    | (L , H)  <br>U’s yield is low (few fields); D enjoys high yield because ample water reaches downstream. | (M , M)  <br>Both keep current fields → moderate, balanced yields. |

**Why this is a distinct strategic tension**  
* The two farmers compete for the **same limited water flow**.  
* Their **spatial positions** create an asymmetry: the upstream farmer’s decision directly reduces the quantity that can reach the downstream farmer, while the downstream farmer cannot affect upstream water.  
* The dilemma is a classic **“up‑stream extraction vs. down‑stream equity”** conflict, independent of any fish‑population considerations.

### 2️⃣ Action Situation B – *Up‑stream vs. Down‑stream Water Extraction (Fish‑Population Threshold)*  

In this situation the downstream farmer’s **fish‑catch income** depends on whether the **water volume that reaches the lake** in the reproductive month (May) exceeds a **critical ecological threshold** \(T_{flow}\).  
If the flow is below \(T_{flow}\) larvae die → fish recruitment collapses → downstream fish catch falls to zero.  
The upstream farmer’s irrigation level determines the flow that arrives at the lake.

| **Players** | **Up‑stream farmer (U)** | **Down‑stream farmer (D)** |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Strategies** | **I** – *Increase* fields (high water withdrawal)  <br> **S** – *Stay* (low withdrawal) | **C** – *Rely on fish* (maintain current fishing effort)  <br> **A** – *Shift to agriculture* (reduce fishing effort, rely more on crops) |

#### Payoff Matrix  

|                | D‑C (fish‑reliant) | D‑A (agri‑shift) |
|----------------|--------------------|------------------|
| **U‑I** (high withdrawal) | (H , 0)  <br>U gets high crop profit; D’s fish catch collapses → no fish income (0). | (H , M)  <br>U high crop profit; D compensates by increasing crop area → medium total income. |
| **U‑S** (low withdrawal) | (M , H)  <br>U’s crop profit is moderate; flow stays above threshold → D enjoys high fish income. | (M , M)  <br>Both obtain moderate combined income (U moderate crops, D moderate fish + some crops). |

**Why this is a distinct strategic tension**  
* The **ecological threshold** (minimum flow for larval survival) introduces a **non‑linear, tipping‑point** effect that only appears when the upstream farmer extracts too much water.  
* The downstream farmer now faces a **choice between two livelihood strategies** (fish vs. more agriculture) that are directly contingent on the upstream farmer’s extraction level.  
* This tension is **different** from the pure crop‑yield conflict of Situation A because the downstream payoff is **conditional on an ecosystem state** rather than on the amount of water left for irrigation alone.

### 3️⃣ Action Situation C – *Neighbouring Down‑stream Farmers Competition for Fish (Harvest‑Timing Conflict)*  

Only the **down‑stream segment** (farmers 7‑9) compete for the **finite fish stock** in the lake.  
Because fish are harvested **randomly** from adult age classes, the order of access matters: the **most downstream farmer** (farmer 9) fishes first and can take a larger share, leaving less for the upstream down‑stream neighbours.

| **Players** | **Farmer 9 (most downstream)** | **Farmer 8 (up‑stream neighbour)** |
|-------------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Strategies** | **H** – *High target catch* (attempt to harvest the full quota)  <br> **L** – *Low target catch* (conservative, accept less) | **H** – *High target catch*  <br> **L** – *Low target catch* |

#### Payoff Matrix  

|                | Farmer 8‑H | Farmer 8‑L |
|----------------|------------|------------|
| **Farmer 9‑H** | (H , L)  <br>Farmer 9 secures most of the fish; Farmer 8 gets little. | (M , M)  <br>Farmer 9’s high effort meets a modest stock; Farmer 8 can still obtain a medium catch. |
| **Farmer 9‑L** | (L , H)  <br>Farmer 9 takes little; Farmer 8, being upstream, now faces a larger residual stock → high catch. | (M , M)  <br>Both harvest conservatively → moderate, shared catch. |

**Why this is a distinct strategic tension**  
* It involves **horizontal competition** among downstream farmers rather than the upstream‑downstream water‑allocation conflict.  
* The **spatial order of access** creates an asymmetry: the downstream farmer can “pre‑empt” the resource.  
* The tension is **independent of irrigation decisions** and instead centres on the **common‑pool fish resource**, highlighting a separate dilemma within the same DV system.
