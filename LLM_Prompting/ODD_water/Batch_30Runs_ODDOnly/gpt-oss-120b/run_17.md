## 1. Water‑Extraction Game – Up‑stream vs Down‑stream Farmer  

**Strategic tension** – Both neighbours decide whether to add **one extra irrigated field** for the coming season.  Water flows first to the upstream farmer; any excess extraction reduces the volume that reaches the downstream farm, possibly creating water‑stress and lower yields.

|                              | **Down‑stream: Add field (A)** | **Down‑stream: Do not add (N)** |
|------------------------------|------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Up‑stream: Add field (A)** | (U = +8 , D = ‑2)            | (U = +6 , D = +1)               |
| **Up‑stream: Do not add (N)**| (U = +1 , D = +6)            | (U = +3 , D = +3)               |

*Payoff units = net profit (budget‑adjusted yield) for the season.*  

**Interpretation / justification**  

* **A‑A** – Both farms push the water demand above the expected inflow (W).  The upstream farmer still receives enough water to fill the extra field (high profit +8), but the downstream farmer suffers a shortfall (‑2).  
* **A‑N** – Only the upstream farmer expands; enough water remains for the downstream farm to meet its original demand (small gain +1).  
* **N‑A** – The downstream farmer expands while the upstream farm stays at baseline; water left after the upstream withdrawal is sufficient for the downstream extra field (down‑stream profit +6) and the upstream farmer keeps a modest profit (+1).  
* **N‑N** – No one expands; both operate under safe water levels (moderate, equal profit +3).  

The matrix captures the **asymmetric impact of the same action** (adding a field) on the two players because of the directional flow of water.

## 2. Fishing‑Access Game – Down‑stream vs Up‑stream Farmer  

**Strategic tension** – The lake is visited sequentially; the downstream farmer fishes first.  Each farmer chooses either to **fish at the target catch (T)** or to **restrict catch (R)** (e.g., keep part of the stock for future seasons).  The stock is age‑structured; heavy early extraction can push the adult‑fish population below the recruitment threshold, a **tipping point** for future fish availability.

|                              | **Up‑stream: Target (T)** | **Up‑stream: Restrict (R)** |
|------------------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Down‑stream: Target (T)**  | (D = ‑4 , U = ‑4)         | (D = +2 , U = ‑2)           |
| **Down‑stream: Restrict (R)**| (D = ‑2 , U = +2)         | (D = +3 , U = +3)           |

*Payoff units = net budget impact from fishing (positive = food/consumption value, negative = loss of future stock).*

**Interpretation / justification**  

* **T‑T** – Both harvest the full target.  The downstream farmer removes the first batch, leaving a depleted stock for the upstream farmer; both incur a future‑stock penalty (‑4 each).  
* **T‑R** – Downstream harvests heavily; upstream conserves.  Downstream gains (+2) but the stock is already stressed, so upstream suffers a small loss (‑2).  
* **R‑T** – Downstream restrains, leaving more fish for the upstream farmer who then takes the target.  Downstream loses a little (‑2) while upstream gains (+2).  
* **R‑R** – Both practice restraint; the adult population stays above the recruitment threshold, so each enjoys a modest net benefit (+3).  

The game makes explicit the **sequential, spatial asymmetry** (downstream moves first) and the **ecological threshold** (if total catch > threshold, future recruitment collapses, reflected by the negative payoffs in the two “T‑T” cells).

## 3. Field‑Expansion Coordination Game – Two Neighbouring Farmers  

**Strategic tension** – Each farmer decides whether to **experimentally increase the number of irrigated fields by one** (strategy **Expand E**) or to **maintain the current field count** (strategy **Hold H**).  The decision is risky because a failed water prediction can cause a **budget shortfall** for the farmer that expands, while the other farmer’s outcome depends on the remaining water.

|                              | **Neighbour 2: Expand (E)** | **Neighbour 2: Hold (H)** |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Neighbour 1: Expand (E)**  | (1 = ‑3 , 2 = ‑3)           | (1 = ‑1 , 2 = +2)         |
| **Neighbour 1: Hold (H)**    | (1 = +2 , 2 = ‑1)           | (1 = +1 , 2 = +1)         |

*Payoff units = change in end‑of‑year budget relative to the “hold‑hold” baseline.*

**Interpretation / justification**  

* **E‑E** – Both expand; total water demand exceeds the expected inflow, causing water stress for both farms.  Each suffers a budget loss (‑3).  
* **E‑H** – Farmer 1 expands while farmer 2 holds.  Farmer 1’s extra field is supplied (small loss due to risk, ‑1), farmer 2 enjoys a surplus water flow and gains (+2).  
* **H‑E** – Symmetric to the previous cell.  
* **H‑H** – No one takes the risk; both keep a modest positive change (+1) because water is used efficiently.

The game isolates the **strategic dilemma of individual risk‑taking versus collective stability** that emerges only under decentralised decision‑making.

## 4. Water‑Threshold Game – Farmer vs Ecological State  

**Strategic tension** – The farmer’s **extraction level** (High H vs Low L) interacts with the **environmental water‑flow state** (Above the larvae‑migration threshold **A**, or Below it **B**).  The environmental “player” is not a decision‑maker but is represented as a contingent state that determines whether fish recruitment occurs (a true ecological tipping point).

|                              | **Environment: Above Threshold (A)** | **Environment: Below Threshold (B)** |
|------------------------------|---------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Farmer: Low extraction (L)**| (F = +5 , E = +4)                     | (F = +2 , E = ‑2)                     |
| **Farmer: High extraction (H)**| (F = +1 , E = ‑3)                     | (F = ‑4 , E = ‑5)                     |

*F = farmer’s net profit (crop + fish value); E = ecological payoff (future fish recruitment, expressed as a proxy “stock health” score).*

**Interpretation / justification**  

* When the flow stays **above** the migration threshold (A), even a high extraction leaves enough water for larvae; the farmer still earns a modest profit (+1) but the ecosystem loses some health (‑3) because less water reaches the lake.  
* With **low extraction** under A, the farmer enjoys a higher profit (+5) and the ecosystem remains healthy (+4).  
* If the flow falls **below** the threshold (B) because of natural variability, low extraction still yields a reduced profit (+2) and harms the ecosystem (‑2).  
* High extraction when the flow is already below the threshold collapses both the farmer’s yield (‑4) and the fish recruitment (‑5).  

This matrix makes the **ecological tipping point** explicit: the farmer’s choice can push the system from A to B, and the payoff to the farmer is highly contingent on the environmental state.
