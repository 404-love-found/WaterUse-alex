### Action Situation 1: Asymmetric Water Appropriation Dilemma

**Strategic Tension**
The physical layout of the river grants upstream farmers first-mover advantage in water extraction. The strategic tension lies in the upstream farmer’s incentive to maximize agricultural output (up to the limit of 10 fields) versus the downstream farmer’s vulnerability to severe water stress. Because water flows sequentially, the upstream farmer's appropriation directly dictates the downstream farmer's available resource, creating an asymmetric commons dilemma where the upstream agent can unilaterally impose water deficits on the downstream agent.

**2-Player Normal Form Payoff Matrix**
*Payoffs represent relative agricultural yield/survival (Upstream Farmer, Downstream Farmer). Max fields = 10.*

| Upstream Farmer \ Downstream Farmer | Maximize (10 fields) | Conserve (5 fields) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Maximize (10 fields)** | (8, 2) | (8, 5) |
| **Conserve (5 fields)** | (5, 8) | (6, 6) |

**Justification**
This situation isolates the spatial asymmetry of water flow in the decentralized version (DV). The upstream farmer has a dominant strategy to maximize fields (8 > 5), knowing they receive the water first. The downstream farmer faces a harsh reality: if the upstream farmer maximizes, the downstream farmer's yield drops drastically (2) if they also try to maximize 10 fields due to water stress, but they can mitigate the damage by conserving (5). The tension highlights how spatial positioning in a river basin structurally disadvantages downstream actors in a decentralized water appropriation game.

### Action Situation 2: Ecological Threshold and Fishery Collapse Dilemma

**Strategic Tension**
The biological viability of the fishing lake depends on a strict ecological threshold: water inflow must exceed a minimum level for fish larvae to migrate and survive. The upstream farmer’s irrigation decisions act as a "kill switch" for the downstream farmer’s fishing livelihood. The tension arises from the upstream farmer’s pursuit of maximum agricultural yield (10 fields) risking a breach of this tipping point. If the threshold is crossed, the fishery collapses, severely degrading the downstream farmer's payoffs regardless of their fishing efforts, creating a profound inter-sectoral spatial externality.

**2-Player Normal Form Payoff Matrix**
*Payoffs represent combined utility from agriculture and fishing (Upstream Farmer, Downstream Farmer). Max fields = 10.*

| Upstream Farmer \ Downstream Farmer | Overfish (Max Target) | Sustain Fish (Min Target) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Max Agri (10 fields)** *(Breaches Threshold)* | (10, 2) | (10, 4) |
| **Conserve Agri (6 fields)** *(Maintains Threshold)* | (6, 7) | (6, 9) |

**Justification**
This situation explicitly models the ecological threshold constraint. The upstream farmer's choice determines whether the biophysical tipping point is crossed. If the upstream farmer maximizes irrigation to 10 fields, water inflow drops below the threshold, causing fish larvae mortality and collapsing the fishery (downstream payoffs drop to 2 or 4). The tension is that the upstream farmer captures all the marginal agricultural benefits (10) while externalizing the ecological cost onto the downstream farmer. The downstream farmer's choice (Overfish vs. Sustain) only meaningfully impacts their payoff if the upstream farmer conserves water to maintain the ecological threshold.

### Action Situation 3: Spatial Priority Access Fishing Dilemma

**Strategic Tension**
Among the downstream farmers, spatial proximity to the lake dictates the order of access to the fishery. The strategic tension is an asymmetric commons dilemma driven by physical priority. The farmer closest to the lake (Downstream Farmer 1) has a structural advantage and a dominant strategy to over-exploit the resource. The second farmer (Downstream Farmer 2) is relegated to a subordinate position; they cannot sustain the resource if Farmer 1 overfishes, and are perversely incentivized to exploit Farmer 1's restraint if Farmer 1 chooses to conserve.

**2-Player Normal Form Payoff Matrix**
*Payoffs represent fish catch utility (Downstream Farmer 1, Downstream Farmer 2).*

| Downstream Farmer 1 (Closer) \ Downstream Farmer 2 (Further) | Overfish (Target Max) | Conserve (Target Min) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Overfish (Target Max)** | (8, 1) | (8, 2) |
| **Conserve (Target Min)** | (4, 7) | (5, 5) |

**Justification**
This situation reflects the spatial asymmetry in fishing access described in the model ("downstream farmers can access the lake first"). Downstream Farmer 1 has a dominant strategy to Overfish (8 > 5, and 8 > 4). Downstream Farmer 2's best response is highly dependent on Farmer 1: if Farmer 1 overfishes, Farmer 2 is left with scraps (1 or 2), making their choice largely irrelevant to the overall collapse; if Farmer 1 conserves, Farmer 2 is incentivized to Overfish (7 > 5) to capture the surplus. This creates an unstable, inequitable equilibrium where the physical proximity to the common-pool resource dictates extraction power, undermining collective sustainability.
