# Run 16 — Qwen/Qwen3.7-Plus

### Action Situation 1: Asymmetric Water Appropriation for Crop Irrigation

**Title:** 
Spatially Asymmetric Common-Pool Resource Appropriation

**Tension:** 
In the decentralized version (DV), farmers independently decide how many fields to irrigate (up to a maximum of 10). Because water flows sequentially from upstream to downstream, upstream farmers possess a distinct physical advantage: first access to the river's flow. The strategic tension arises from the upstream farmer's individual incentive to maximize agricultural output by extracting as much water as possible, which directly imposes a severe negative externality on the downstream farmer. The downstream farmer faces water stress and reduced crop yields but lacks the spatial power to retaliate or restrict the upstream farmer, creating a highly asymmetric exploitation dilemma.

**2-Player Normal Form Payoff Matrix:**
*(Payoffs represent relative crop yields/budget accumulation. Row Player = Upstream Farmer, Column Player = Downstream Farmer)*

| Upstream \ Downstream | Conserve (Irrigate 5 fields) | Maximize (Irrigate 10 fields) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Conserve (5 fields)** | 60 , 60 | 40 , 80 |
| **Maximize (10 fields)** | 90 , 20 | 70 , 0 |

**Justification:** 
The payoffs mathematically reflect the spatial asymmetry and the "Max fields = 10" constraint. For the Upstream farmer, "Maximize" is a strictly dominant strategy (90 > 60; 70 > 40) because they receive water first and do not suffer from the downstream farmer's extraction. For the Downstream farmer, the dilemma is structural: if the Upstream farmer conserves, the Downstream farmer is tempted to maximize (80 > 60). However, because the Upstream farmer will rationally choose to maximize, the Downstream farmer's best response is forced into "Conserve" (20 > 0) to avoid total crop failure from severe water stress. This illustrates a Stackelberg-like asymmetry where the upstream actor dictates the outcome, highlighting the spatial injustice inherent in the uncoordinated DV.

### Action Situation 2: Ecological Tipping Point and Fishery Collapse

**Title:** 
Ecological Threshold Breach and Disproportionate Fishery Loss

**Tension:** 
The fish population in the downstream lake is non-viable without a regular inflow of larvae, which only occurs if the water inflow into the lake during the May reproduction period exceeds a critical ecological threshold. In the DV, the water reaching the lake is the residual flow after both upstream and downstream farmers have extracted water for irrigation. The tension arises because individual farmers are incentivized to maximize their irrigated fields (up to 10), but aggregate extraction risks dropping the lake's inflow below the threshold. If breached, the fish population collapses. Because downstream farmers access the lake first and rely on the fish catch for subsistence and investment, they bear the brunt of this ecological collapse, while upstream farmers are largely insulated from the fishery loss.

**2-Player Normal Form Payoff Matrix:**
*(Payoffs represent combined budget returns from crops and fish. Row Player = Upstream Farmer, Column Player = Downstream Farmer)*

| Upstream \ Downstream | Conserve (Leave water for lake) | Defect (Extract max for crops) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Conserve (Leave water)** | 50 , 90 | 50 , 60 |
| **Defect (Extract max)** | 80 , 20 | 80 , 10 |

**Justification:** 
This situation explicitly models the ecological threshold constraint. If both conserve, the May inflow threshold is met, the fish survive, and the Downstream farmer receives a high payoff (90) due to the fishery bonus. If either or both defect, aggregate extraction breaches the threshold, causing a total fishery collapse. The payoffs illustrate that the Upstream farmer's utility is unaffected by the fishery collapse (80 vs 80), making "Defect" a strictly dominant strategy for them. For the Downstream farmer, the dilemma is acute: while they prefer ecological stability (90), the short-term temptation to maximize crop fields (60) exists. However, knowing the Upstream farmer will defect, the Downstream farmer is forced to "Conserve" (20 > 10) to avoid the absolute worst outcome of total crop water stress combined with fishery collapse. This reflects a tragedy of the commons bounded by a non-linear ecological tipping point, where spatial asymmetry again severely punishes the downstream actor.
