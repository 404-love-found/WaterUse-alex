## 1.  Water‑extraction Competition (Up‑stream vs. Down‑stream)

|                     | **Down‑stream: Low (4 fields)** | **Down‑stream: High (8 fields)** |
|---------------------|--------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Up‑stream: Low (4 fields)** | **(U = 13, D = 13)**<br>Both farms keep enough water; river flow stays **above the larval‑migration threshold** → healthy fish stock. Each farmer gets 8 units of crop yield + 5 units of fish income. | **(U = 9, D = 16)**<br>Up‑stream extracts little, leaving sufficient water for the downstream farm to irrigate 8 fields. Both receive the ecological‑threshold benefit. Up‑stream’s yield falls to 4 units (only 4 fields) + 5 fish = 9; downstream gets 12 yield + 4 fish = 16. |
| **Up‑stream: High (8 fields)** | **(U = 16, D = 8)**<br>Up‑stream takes most water but still leaves enough for downstream to irrigate its 4 fields. River flow **remains above the threshold** (≤ 6 fields extracted upstream). Up‑stream gets 12 yield + 4 fish = 16; downstream 4 yield + 4 fish = 8. | **(U = 12, D = 2)**<br>Both farms try to irrigate 8 fields. Up‑stream’s extraction pushes the **river‑flow below the larval‑migration threshold** → no larvae enter the lake, fish stock collapses. Up‑stream still harvests its crop (12 yield) but receives **0 fish** → 12. Down‑stream receives only the water left after the upstream draw (≈2 units of water → very low yield) and **0 fish** → 2. |

### Why this is a distinct strategic tension
* **Players:** an upstream farmer (U) and a downstream farmer (D).  
* **Conflict:** each decides how many fields to irrigate (high vs. low).  
* **Spatial asymmetry:** water is allocated sequentially from upstream to downstream, so U’s decision directly constrains D’s water availability.  
* **Ecological threshold:** if upstream extraction exceeds the threshold (≈ 6 fields), river flow drops below the level required for larval migration, eliminating the fish resource for both farms.  
* **Strategic dilemma:** the Pareto‑optimal outcome is (Low, Low) (both keep the ecosystem functional), but each farmer has an incentive to “defect” to High to boost his own crop revenue, risking the ecological tipping point and harming the downstream neighbour.

## 2.  Fishing‑effort Competition (Down‑stream vs. Up‑stream)

|                     | **Up‑stream: Low (≤ target catch)** | **Up‑stream: High (= target catch)** |
|---------------------|-------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Down‑stream: Low** | **(U = 4, D = 4)**<br>Both fish conservatively; the fish stock stays **above the sustainable level**. Each farmer harvests 4 units of fish (no cost). | **(U = 6, D = 4)**<br>Down‑stream holds back, leaving enough fish for the upstream farmer to take the full target (6 units). Stock remains viable. |
| **Down‑stream: High** | **(U = 2, D = 6)**<br>Down‑stream fishes first and captures the whole target (6 units). Only a small residual (2 units) is left for the upstream farmer. Stock is still above the long‑term sustainability line because total catch ≤ threshold. | **(U = 0, D = 6)**<br>Both attempt the full target. Because the downstream farmer accesses the lake first, he secures the entire target (6 units) and the upstream farmer gets nothing. The **combined catch exceeds the sustainable harvest level**, pushing the fish population toward the density‑dependent mortality regime (future stock collapse). |

### Why this is a distinct strategic tension
* **Players:** downstream farmer (who has priority access) and upstream farmer (who fishes after).  
* **Conflict:** each chooses a fishing intensity – **Low** (conserve) or **High** (take the full target).  
* **Spatial asymmetry:** priority access gives the downstream farmer a first‑mover advantage; the upstream farmer can only take what remains.  
* **Ecological threshold:** the fish population can sustain a total annual catch up to a **harvest‑sustainability threshold** (here ≈ 8 units). The (High, High) cell exceeds that limit, creating a “tipping point” that will erode future fish abundance (implicit in the payoff of 0 for the upstream farmer).  
* **Strategic dilemma:** the (Low, Low) outcome is jointly optimal for long‑term fish abundance, but each farmer can improve his immediate payoff by unilaterally moving to **High** (especially the downstream farmer). The result is a classic Prisoner’s‑Dilemma shaped by spatial priority and an ecological tipping point.

## 3.  Up‑stream Water‑Extraction vs. Down‑stream Fish‑Harvest (Cross‑resource Interaction)

|                     | **Down‑stream: Low (≤ target catch)** | **Down‑stream: High (= target catch)** |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Up‑stream: Low (4 fields)** | **(U = 9, D = 13)**<br>Up‑stream extracts modest water → river flow stays above the larval‑migration threshold, so fish recruitment is healthy. Down‑stream keeps a low catch; both enjoy decent crop yields (U = 4 fields → 8 yield) and fish income (U = 5, D = 5). Total pay‑offs: U = 13, D = 13 → after subtracting the low catch cost D = 13‑0 = 13 (shown as 13). |
| **Up‑stream: High (8 fields)** | **(U = 12, D = 2)**<br>Up‑stream’s heavy extraction pushes flow **below the larval‑migration threshold** → fish recruitment fails. Down‑stream, even with a low catch, gets **zero fish**. Up‑stream still receives crop yield 12 + 0 fish = 12; downstream gets only a tiny crop yield (2) and no fish. | **(U = 12, D = 0)**<br>Up‑stream extracts heavily (threshold breached) and down‑stream also attempts a high catch. Because the fish stock has collapsed, both receive **no fish**; downstream’s crop yield is also starved (2 units) → payoff 0 for D, 12 for U. |

### Why this is a distinct strategic tension
* **Players:** upstream farmer (water‑extraction decision) and downstream farmer (fish‑harvest decision).  
* **Conflict across resources:** the upstream farmer’s water use determines whether the ecological water‑flow threshold is met, which in turn governs the availability of fish for the downstream farmer.  
* **Spatial asymmetry:** the upstream action **unilaterally** affects the downstream resource (fish) while the downstream farmer cannot influence upstream water use.  
* **Ecological threshold:** the **larval‑migration water‑flow threshold** is a hard tipping point; once crossed, fish recruitment collapses, eliminating the downstream farmer’s fish income regardless of his own fishing effort.  
* **Strategic dilemma:** the downstream farmer would like the upstream farmer to keep extraction low (cooperate) to preserve fish, but the upstream farmer gains a higher crop yield by extracting heavily. The matrix shows a clear **dominant strategy** for the upstream farmer (High) and a **dominant strategy** for the downstream farmer (Low) when the upstream farmer extracts heavily, illustrating a cross‑resource “tragedy of the commons” situation.
