**Normal‑Form Game (Up‑stream U vs Down‑stream D)**  

|                     | **Down‑stream: Hold (H)** | **Down‑stream: Expand (E)** |
|---------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Up‑stream: Hold (H)** | (4, 4) – Both keep current fields, water enough for both; yields = 4 units each. | (5, 2) – Up‑stream holds, down‑stream expands, water left for up‑stream is unchanged, down‑stream suffers water stress → lower yield. |
| **Up‑stream: Expand (E)** | (2, 5) – Up‑stream expands, down‑stream holds; upstream experiences water stress, down‑stream receives enough water → higher yield downstream. | (1, 1) – Both expand; upstream extracts most water, downstream receives very little → severe water stress for both, yields collapse. |

*Payoffs are simplified yield units (higher = better).  The matrix reflects the spatial asymmetry: the upstream farmer’s expansion directly harms the downstream farmer, while the downstream farmer’s expansion only harms itself (because water has already been taken upstream).*

**Justification**  
- The decision to **Expand** is limited by the 10‑field cap and by the farmer’s budget, but in the game we abstract to a binary choice each season.  
- Water is withdrawn sequentially; therefore the upstream farmer’s extra field reduces the volume that reaches the downstream neighbour – a classic *upstream‑downstream* externality.  
- The payoffs capture the **strategic tension** “*Should I expand my irrigation at the risk of depriving my downstream neighbour?*”.

## 2.  Fishing‑Access Competition – “Down‑stream Priority vs Up‑stream Catch”

| **Strategic tension** | Down‑stream farmer decides whether to **Fish Aggressively** (target catch = high) or **Fish Conservatively** (target = low). Up‑stream farmer, who fishes later, chooses the same two alternatives.  Because the lake is accessed first by the downstream farmer, his harvest reduces the stock available to the upstream farmer, creating a *sequential harvest* dilemma. |

**Normal‑Form Game (Down‑stream D vs Up‑stream U)**  

|                     | **Up‑stream: Conserv. (C)** | **Up‑stream: Aggressive (A)** |
|---------------------|-----------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Down‑stream: Conserv. (C)** | (3, 3) – Both fish lightly; fish stock stays above the ecological threshold → sustainable yields. | (2, 4) – Down‑stream conserves, up‑stream over‑exploits; stock drops but down‑stream still gets enough because he fishes first. |
| **Down‑stream: Aggressive (A)** | (4, 2) – Down‑stream over‑exploits, up‑stream conserves; stock falls below the **larval‑migration threshold** for the next year, reducing future fish recruitment → down‑stream gets short‑term gain, up‑stream suffers later. | (1, 1) – Both over‑exploit; stock collapses below the ecological tipping point, future catches near zero for both. |

*Payoffs are “utility” units that combine immediate fish catch and expected future stock health (penalising outcomes that push the fish population below the larval‑migration water‑threshold).*

**Justification**  
- The lake is accessed **first** by the downstream farmer, giving him a positional advantage.  
- The **ecological threshold** (minimum water flow in May required for larval survival) couples the harvest to future stock health; if total catch is too high, the stock may fall below the density‑dependent mortality tipping point, causing a collapse.  
- The strategic tension is “*Should I fish aggressively now, risking a future collapse that will also hurt my downstream neighbour?*”.

## 3.  Budget‑Driven Field Expansion vs Ecological Water‑Threshold

| **Strategic tension** | A single farmer (any position) decides whether to **Push Expansion** (add one field, up to the 10‑field cap) or **Stay Safe** (keep current fields). The *opponent* is the **Water‑Flow Regime**, which can be **High** (annual inflow above the larval‑migration threshold) or **Low** (below the threshold).  The farmer’s payoff depends on both his budget constraint and the ecological water‑threshold that determines whether the fish population (and thus the supplementary subsistence income) survives.  This captures the *risk‑taking vs environmental uncertainty* dilemma that is present for every farmer but is modelled as a 2‑player game for analytical clarity. |

**Normal‑Form Game (Farmer F vs Water‑Regime W)**  

|                     | **Water Regime: High (H)** – annual inflow ≥ larval‑migration threshold | **Water Regime: Low (L)** – annual inflow < threshold |
|---------------------|-------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Farmer: Stay Safe (S)** | (5, 5) – Farmer keeps ≤ 5 fields, budget stays positive; high water guarantees fish recruitment → supplemental income, total utility high. | (4, 5) – Farmer stays safe, still gets modest yields; low water reduces fish stock but farmer’s low water demand avoids severe stress. |
| **Farmer: Push Expansion (P)** | (7, 4) – Farmer expands to 6‑10 fields, gains extra agricultural income; water still abundant, fish stock remains healthy → overall high utility. | (2, 2) – Farmer expands despite low water; severe water stress collapses yields, fish recruitment fails → both farmer and ecosystem suffer (tipping point). |

*Utility combines (agricultural yield + expected fish‑derived income − irrigation cost).  The “Water‑Regime” player’s payoff is a proxy for ecosystem health (high = fish population above threshold, low = population collapsed).*

**Justification**  
- The **ecological threshold** is the minimum May‑flow needed for larval survival; when the regime is **Low**, the fish stock cannot replenish, removing the subsistence safety net for the farmer.  
- The farmer’s **Push Expansion** decision is limited by the 10‑field cap and by his budget; in a high‑flow year the risk is bearable, but in a low‑flow year it pushes the system past the tipping point.  
- The strategic tension is “*Should I expand my fields now, risking a water‑scarcity year that could push the fish population below its ecological threshold?*”
