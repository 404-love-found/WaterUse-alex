### 1. DSM Coordination Game (Capacitor Adoption)
**1. Title:** DSM Coordination Game (Capacitor Adoption)
**2. Location:** Transformer group level (village-level shared infrastructure).
**3. Players:** Two farmers sharing the same transformer.
**4. Roles:** Electricity consumer, potential Demand-Side Management (DSM) adopter.
**5. Actions:** Invest in capacitor, Do not invest.
**6. Control Rules:** The shared benefit of voltage stabilization and pump efficiency is only realized if a threshold of farmers on the transformer invest simultaneously. If the threshold is not met, the investor bears the cost with no return.
**7. Information:** Partial and noisy. Farmers observe past voltage quality and peer adoption but often misinterpret technical causes due to incomplete knowledge.
**8. Outcomes:** Capacitor installed (or not), local voltage quality change, individual budget depletion.
**9. Payoffs:** Investors pay a private financial cost. If the collective threshold is met, all farmers on the transformer gain reliability benefits.
**10. Strategic Tension:** **Strategic (Assurance/Coordination Game).** The tension lies between the individual cost of investment and the need for collective action to realize benefits. Farmers face the risk of being the "sucker" who pays for an upgrade that fails to reach the adoption threshold.
**11. Temporal Structure:** Repeated annually (strategic decisions made once per year).
**12. Relevant Rules:** *Boundary rules* restrict the game to farmers on the same transformer; *choice rules* allow investing or not; *control rules* dictate that benefits require simultaneous collective adoption.

**Payoff Matrix (Ordinal 0–3):**
| Farmer 1 \ Farmer 2 | Invest (I) | Not Invest (N) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Invest (I)** | 3, 3 | 0, 2 |
| **Not Invest (N)** | 2, 0 | 1, 1 |

*Explanation:* (I,I) yields 3,3 as both pay the cost but gain the shared reliability benefit. (I,N) yields 0,2 because the threshold fails; the investor gets no benefit (0) while the non-investor free-rides on any minor localized effects (2). (N,N) yields 1,1 as the status quo is maintained with no costs or benefits.

### 2. Collusion Exchange Game (Informal Tie Formation)
**1. Title:** Collusion Exchange Game (Informal Tie Formation)
**2. Location:** Substation / local informal social network.
**3. Players:** One farmer, one substation staff member.
**4. Roles:** Electricity consumer, utility service provider/enforcer.
**5. Actions:** Collude (offer/accept informal favor), Do not collude.
**6. Control Rules:** A collusive tie forms only if both parties independently agree. If formed, the farmer receives informal benefits (e.g., ignored unauthorized use), and the staff receives reciprocal benefits (bribes/social capital). Both face a stochastic risk of detection and sanction.
**7. Information:** Partial. Staff knows the farmer's capacity to reciprocate; farmer knows the staff's corruption level. Both face uncertainty regarding monitoring intensity.
**8. Outcomes:** Collusive tie formed or rejected. Informal benefits exchanged or formal rules enforced.
**9. Payoffs:** Mutual informal gain if both collude and avoid detection. High penalty if caught. Safe but lower baseline payoffs if formal rules are followed.
**10. Strategic Tension:** **Strategic (Game of Trust / Coordination).** The tension is between the mutual benefits of informal exchange and the risk of institutional sanction. It requires mutual trust; if one party offers and the other refuses, the offering party risks exposure without gain.
**11. Temporal Structure:** Repeated annually (matching and negotiation occur once per year).
**12. Relevant Rules:** *Position rules* assign discretionary power to staff; *choice rules* allow colluding or not; *control rules* require mutual agreement and impose stochastic detection penalties.

**Payoff Matrix (Ordinal 0–3):**
| Farmer \ Staff | Collude (C) | Not Collude (NC) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Collude (C)** | 3, 3 | 0, 1 |
| **Not Collude (NC)** | 1, 0 | 2, 2 |

*Explanation:* (C,C) yields 3,3 as both gain informal benefits net of detection risk. (C,NC) yields 0,1 because the farmer risks rejection/exposure (0) while the staff safely maintains the status quo (1). (NC,NC) yields 2,2 as both rely on formal rules, avoiding risk but missing informal perks.

### 3. Capacity Provision Game (Transformer Upgrade & Regularisation)
**1. Title:** Capacity Provision Game (Transformer Upgrade & Regularisation)
**2. Location:** Transformer group / substation.
**3. Players:** Substation staff, tied farmer.
**4. Roles:** Utility allocator/investor, electricity consumer/free-rider.
**5. Actions:** Staff: Invest in capacity, Do not invest. Farmer: Pay regularisation fee, Do not pay.
**6. Control Rules:** Upgrading transformer capacity requires staff effort, which is constrained by their workload. Formalising the connection requires the farmer to pay a fee. If staff invest but the farmer doesn't pay, the staff bears the effort cost while the farmer free-rides on the upgraded capacity.
**7. Information:** Partial. Staff knows their workload and the farmer's tie strength; farmer knows the capacity upgrade status.
**8. Outcomes:** Transformer capacity increased or not. Farmer formally connected or remains informal.
**9. Payoffs:** Staff bears effort costs; farmer bears financial costs. Both benefit from reliable capacity, but the distribution of costs creates an asymmetric dilemma.
**10. Strategic Tension:** **Strategic (Asymmetric Public Goods / Conflict Game).** The tension arises from uneven cost-bearing. The farmer prefers the staff to invest regardless of whether the farmer pays, while the staff will only invest if the farmer pays to compensate for the effort. This asymmetry often leads to under-provision of capacity.
**11. Temporal Structure:** Repeated annually.
**12. Relevant Rules:** *Boundary rules* restrict to tied farmer-staff pairs; *choice rules* allow investing/not and paying/not; *control rules* link capacity provision to staff effort and formalisation to farmer payment.

**Payoff Matrix (Ordinal 0–3):**
| Staff \ Farmer | Pay (P) | Not Pay (NP) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Invest (I)** | 2, 3 | 0, 2 |
| **Not Invest (NI)** | 3, 0 | 1, 1 |

*Explanation:* (I,P) yields 2,3 as both cooperate; staff gets compensated for effort (2), farmer gets capacity (3). (I,NP) yields 0,2 as staff wastes effort (0) while farmer free-rides (2). (NI,P) yields 3,0 as staff saves effort but takes the fee without delivering capacity (3), while farmer pays for nothing (0). (NI,NP) yields 1,1 as the status quo is maintained.

### 4. Groundwater Extraction Game (Aquifer Depletion)
**1. Title:** Groundwater Extraction Game (Aquifer Depletion)
**2. Location:** Village-level groundwater basin / shared aquifer.
**3. Players:** Two connected farmers sharing the same aquifer.
**4. Roles:** Groundwater extractor, electricity consumer.
**5. Actions:** Pump at full rate, Restrain extraction.
**6. Control Rules:** Total extraction determines aquifer drawdown. As the aquifer depletes, the energy cost of pumping rises dynamically. Restraint by one farmer benefits both by lowering future costs, but full pumping maximizes short-term individual yield.
**7. Information:** Partial and noisy. Farmers observe groundwater depth and pumping costs but may misattribute the causes of drawdown.
**8. Outcomes:** Aquifer level change, pumping energy cost change, individual crop yield.
**9. Payoffs:** Trade-off between short-term agricultural yield and long-term pumping energy costs.
**10. Strategic Tension:** **Strategic (Common Pool Resource / Prisoner’s Dilemma).** The classic tragedy of the commons. The tension is between the individual short-term gain from full extraction and the collective long-term benefit of aquifer restraint. Individual rationality leads to collective ecological degradation.
**11. Temporal Structure:** Continuous/Repeated (decisions made annually, but physical drawdown computed every monthly tick).
**12. Relevant Rules:** *Boundary rules* define the shared aquifer; *choice rules* allow full or restrained extraction; *control rules* dictate that aggregate extraction drives aquifer depletion and future cost increases.

**Payoff Matrix (Ordinal 0–3):**
| Farmer 1 \ Farmer 2 | Restrain (R) | Full (F) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Restrain (R)** | 2, 2 | 0, 3 |
| **Full (F)** | 3, 0 | 1, 1 |

*Explanation:* (R,R) yields 2,2 as the aquifer remains stable with moderate yields and low pumping costs. (R,F) yields 0,3 because F1 restrains while F2 pumps fully; F2 gets high short-term yield (3) while F1 suffers from drawdown and high costs (0). (F,F) yields 1,1 as both pump fully, depleting the aquifer and driving up energy costs, resulting in lower net yields for both.

### 5. Social Learning Game (Observation and Imitation)
**1. Title:** Social Learning Game (Observation and Imitation)
**2. Location:** Transformer group / village social network.
**3. Players:** Individual farmers (modeled sequentially).
**4. Roles:** Observer, imitator, experimenter.
**5. Actions:** Observe neighbors, Experiment (adopt without prior success), Imitate (adopt after seeing neighbors succeed), Do not adopt.
**6. Control Rules:** Non-strategic sequential process. A small number of farmers act as "experimenters" regardless of outcomes. The imitation pool for others only opens once a threshold of simultaneous adoptions is observed on the transformer. Eligible farmers imitate at a fixed yearly probability.
**7. Information:** Noisy/Erroneous. Farmers observe visible adoption but misinterpret effects on performance due to bounded rationality and incomplete technical understanding.
**8. Outcomes:** Individual adoption status changes, local adoption density increases.
**9. Payoffs:** Experiential payoffs (not strategic). Successful adoption yields reliability benefits; failed sequential adoption yields sunk costs.
**10. Strategic Tension:** **Non-strategic (Sequential Learning Process).** There is no interpersonal strategic dilemma. The "tension" is cognitive and behavioral, driven by bounded rationality, erroneous perception of technical causes, and the stochastic nature of experiential learning.
**11. Temporal Structure:** Continuous/Sequential (happens throughout the year, updated annually).
**12. Relevant Rules:** *Boundary rules* restrict observation to the local transformer network; *choice rules* allow experimenting, imitating, or waiting; *control rules* dictate that neighbor adoption thresholds trigger imitation eligibility.

### Strategic Analysis and Comparison

**Strategic Cores:**
1. **DSM Coordination:** Assurance/Coordination Game. Driven by threshold externalities.
2. **Collusion Exchange:** Game of Trust. Driven by mutual informal benefit vs. institutional risk.
3. **Capacity Provision:** Asymmetric Public Goods. Driven by uneven cost-bearing and free-riding incentives.
4. **Groundwater Extraction:** Prisoner’s Dilemma (CPR). Driven by individual short-term gain vs. collective long-term resource sustainability.

**Comparison and Diversity Check:**
While the **Collusion Exchange** and **Capacity Provision** games both involve interactions between a farmer and substation staff, their strategic cores and incentive structures are fundamentally distinct. Collusion is a symmetric coordination problem based on trust and risk avoidance, whereas Capacity Provision is an asymmetric conflict over who bears the physical/financial costs of infrastructure upgrades. 

Similarly, while **DSM Coordination** and **Groundwater Extraction** both involve two farmers, DSM is an Assurance game where mutual cooperation is the dominant equilibrium if trust is established, whereas Groundwater Extraction is a Prisoner’s Dilemma where mutual defection (full extraction) is the inevitable Nash equilibrium.
